\textbf{Note.} Model answers are deliberately concise. Where a question is open (the disclosure
email, the SBOM essay) the rubric hint states what must be present for full marks. Students may
give defensible alternatives to the CWEs, controls, and timelines below; mark on reasoning,
correct CWE \emph{class}, and source hygiene -- not on exact identifier matches.

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 1.1 -- Lawful sourcing and chain of custody.}\\[2pt]
(a)~Contract download: lawful -- security testing and interoperability RE is broadly permitted
(EU Directive 2009/24/EC; CRA research provisions), and a maintenance contract gives explicit
entitlement. (b)~Own-device SPI dump: lawful -- you own the hardware; note DMCA \S1201
security-research exemption (US) and that in Germany StGB \S202a/202c concern \emph{unauthorised}
access, which does not apply to your own device. (c)~Forwarded ``from a customer site'': do
\emph{not} analyse -- no provenance, no entitlement; a finding from it is contaminated and no
PSIRT or CERT channel will accept it.\\[2pt]
Minimum chain-of-custody metadata: source URL or physical origin, download/read date-time in
UTC, vendor-published hash, your computed hash, and the entitlement basis. A missing element
(e.g.\ no recorded source) means you cannot prove the image is the genuine shipping artefact, so
the vendor can dismiss the report as ``not our firmware''.\\[2pt]
A \emph{hash} only proves the file matches a value the vendor also published on the same
(possibly compromised) channel -- it detects truncation/corruption, not a coordinated mirror
swap. A detached \emph{signature} (vendor private key) proves \emph{authenticity}: a tampered
mirror cannot forge it without the key. The signature defends against the tampered mirror.\\[2pt]
\emph{Rubric hint:} full marks require rejecting (c), and the hash-vs-signature distinction
(integrity vs.\ authenticity).
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 1.2 -- Architecture and endianness triage.}\\[2pt]
(1)~Opcode-frequency scanners (\texttt{binwalk -A}, \texttt{cpu\_rec}) hit the wrong ISA because
many byte patterns are valid-looking instructions in several encodings; data, strings, and
compressed regions also produce spurious matches. Decide by \emph{density and locality}: the
real ISA shows a dense, contiguous run over the code section, not a thin scatter. Cross-checks:
(i)~read the \texttt{uImage}/\texttt{vmlinux} header CPU field if present; (ii)~inspect the
physical chip marking on the board; (iii)~look at the ELF \texttt{e\_machine} of carved
user-space binaries; any two agreeing is decisive.\\[2pt]
(2)~Wrong \emph{endianness} swaps every multi-byte field: instructions decode but operands,
immediates, and branch targets are nonsense, and ASCII strings appear byte-reversed in groups of
four -- a tell-tale ``\texttt{toor}'' for ``\texttt{root}''. Wrong \emph{load/base address} lets
instructions decode correctly but every absolute reference (string xrefs, jump tables, function
pointers) lands in unmapped space, so the listing looks locally sane but all cross-references are
broken. Symptom split: garbled strings $\Rightarrow$ endianness; clean strings but dead xrefs
$\Rightarrow$ base address.\\[2pt]
(3)~The load/base address. With it wrong, Ghidra's absolute references and the entire
cross-reference graph point nowhere, so no auth/parser analysis is trustworthy. (Endianness is
also fatal but is usually fixed by selecting the right language ID at import.)\\[2pt]
\emph{Rubric hint:} accept either base address or endianness for (3) if the reasoning ties to
cross-reference integrity.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 1.3 -- Reading an entropy curve.}\\[2pt]
(1)~\textbf{A} ($\approx0.45$, flat): low entropy = plaintext code/strings or a sparsely-used
filesystem region -- structured, repetitive bytes. \textbf{B} ($\approx0.92$, ramping, flagged
LZMA): high but not maximal, with a recognised compression signature = the compressed kernel or
filesystem. \textbf{C} ($\approx0.99$, flat-maximal, square edges, \emph{no} signature):
near-uniform byte distribution with hard boundaries = encrypted or independently-packed blob.\\[2pt]
(2)~Both good compression and good encryption produce near-uniform output, so Shannon entropy is
indistinguishable between them by construction. The tie-breaker is \emph{structure}: a
compression format leaves a recognisable header/magic and a decompressor can consume it; an
encrypted region has no valid magic and no decompressor accepts it. The absence of a parseable
header at C's offset, plus the square edges, points to encryption/packing.\\[2pt]
(3)~Pivot to the bootloader (or the early init code): something on the device must decrypt C at
boot, so the key and the decryption routine live in a lower-entropy region you \emph{can} read.
Fighting the blob directly (brute force, statistical attack) is the wrong move -- the plaintext
recovery path is to recover the key/algorithm from the code that already holds it.\\[2pt]
\emph{Rubric hint:} the key insight is ``entropy cannot separate compressed from encrypted; the
header/decompressor does'', plus the bootloader pivot.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 1.4 -- Hard-coded secret, no patch.}\\[2pt]
CWEs: (i)~MD5-crypt root hash in \texttt{passwd} -- \textbf{CWE-916} (use of password hash with
insufficient computational effort) compounded by \textbf{CWE-798} if the credential is shared
across units; (ii)~shared device-wide PEM private key -- \textbf{CWE-321} (use of hard-coded
cryptographic key); (iii)~\texttt{SUPER\_PASS=Zte521} compiled in -- \textbf{CWE-798}
(hard-coded credentials), the classic router web-backdoor pattern.\\[2pt]
Compensating controls without a patch:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item (i)~Crackable root hash: deny network access to the management interfaces (SSH/Telnet
        ACL to a jump host on a dedicated management VLAN), and alert on any successful root
        login via syslog forwarding -- the offline crack is unavoidable, so remove the network
        path to use it.
  \item (ii)~Shared TLS key: terminate TLS at a reverse proxy in front of the HMI with a
        per-site certificate and a fresh key, so the shipped key never faces the network;
        monitor for the known fingerprint elsewhere on the segment.
  \item (iii)~Compiled-in master password: a WAF/proxy rule that blocks any request carrying
        that \texttt{Authorization} header or query parameter, plus an IDS signature alerting on
        the literal across the OT segment.
\end{itemize}
Residual-risk ranking (highest first): (iii)~the master password is directly remotely usable and
WAF rules can be evaded by encoding, so residual risk stays highest; (ii)~the shared key still
permits impersonation/MITM if an attacker bypasses the proxy; (i)~the root hash is least, because
once the network path is removed the offline crack has no live target.\\[2pt]
\emph{Rubric hint:} full marks require concrete, named controls (reverse-proxy TLS, WAF
header rule, management-VLAN ACL), not ``segment the network''.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 1.5 -- Reading a Ghidra decompilation.}\\[2pt]
(1)~Line~(1): \texttt{sprintf(path,"/tmp/\%s",basename\_of(url))} into a 64-byte
\texttt{path} with an attacker-influenced URL -- \textbf{CWE-787 / CWE-121} (stack buffer
overflow from unbounded \texttt{sprintf}); the same line also enables \textbf{CWE-22} path
traversal if \texttt{basename\_of} is weak. Line~(2): the update is accepted on a \emph{CRC-32}
match against a value taken from the same attacker-supplied header -- \textbf{CWE-345 /
CWE-347} (missing/improper verification of update authenticity; CRC is an integrity checksum,
not a signature, and the attacker controls both the file and its CRC).\\[2pt]
(2)~The CRC-only update check (line~2/3) is more severe for an Internet-exposed gateway. The
attacker's required position is merely ``reach the update endpoint''; they craft a malicious
firmware, compute its CRC-32 (a public algorithm), put it in the header, and \texttt{flash\_write}
runs it -- full persistent code execution with no key. The \texttt{sprintf} overflow needs a
crash to be turned into reliable RCE and may be mitigated by ASLR/stack canaries; the unsigned
update is a deterministic, position-independent compromise of the whole device.\\[2pt]
(3)~Patch: replace the CRC check in \texttt{apply\_update} with an \emph{asymmetric signature
verification} -- e.g.\ \texttt{verify\_signature("/tmp/fw.bin", sig\_hdr, vendor\_pubkey)} using
an Ed25519/RSA public key baked into the bootloader, verified \emph{before} \texttt{flash\_write},
and enforce a monotonic version floor to block downgrade. CRC stays only as a transport-integrity
check, never as the authenticity gate.\\[2pt]
\emph{Rubric hint:} both CWE classes must appear; the severity argument must turn on
``attacker needs no key, CRC is forgeable'', and the patch must name signature verification +
the bootloader-resident public key, not ``add validation''.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 1.6 -- Static vs.\ emulation.}\\[2pt]
(1)~\textbf{\texttt{qemu-user}}: a statically-linked CGI driven only by environment and stdin has
no hardware or kernel-image dependency, so user-mode CPU emulation runs it transparently -- the
forcing property is ``no device I/O, self-contained binary''.\\[2pt]
(2)~\textbf{\texttt{qemu-user} with the NVRAM read stubbed}, or full-system if stubbing fails.
The forcing property is the \texttt{ioctl} on \texttt{/dev/mtd3}: \texttt{qemu-user} emulates the
CPU only, so the missing device aborts the daemon. Two ways past it without the device: (i)~create
a fake \texttt{/dev/mtd3} / preload a shim library (\texttt{LD\_PRELOAD}) that intercepts the
\texttt{ioctl}/\texttt{open} and returns plausible model/serial bytes; (ii)~patch the binary (NOP
the read or force its return value in Ghidra and re-run) so the start-up check passes. Populating
a faux NVRAM file or using \texttt{QEMU\_LD\_PREFIX} with a stub \texttt{/dev} are accepted
variants.\\[2pt]
(3)~\textbf{Hardware-in-the-loop (Avatar$^2$)} or careful full-system with peripheral models. The
forcing property is the memory-mapped peripheral the auth routine polls: a bare-metal RTOS image
has no OS to host syscall translation, and the peripheral's behaviour (status bits the loop waits
on) cannot be guessed, so you forward those MMIO accesses to the real chip (or a precise model)
while emulating the CPU.\\[2pt]
\emph{Rubric hint:} each answer must name the \emph{one} property (self-contained / device I/O /
MMIO peripheral) that forces the tool, and case~(2) needs two concrete bypasses.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 1.7 -- Version diff and the silent fix.}\\[2pt]
(1)~A two-file delta where the only code change removes a \texttt{strcmp} against a literal
password, with the second file being just \texttt{/etc/version}, is a surgical change confined to
one auth function -- not the broad churn of a feature release or toolchain bump. Removing a
hard-coded credential branch is precisely a security fix; the ``stability improvements''
changelog is the silent-fix tell.\\[2pt]
(2)~The v1.4 backdoor is reportable. First route is still the (now-fixed) vendor PSIRT, because
they must publish an advisory and assign/clear a CVE so v1.4 users know to upgrade; in parallel,
request a CVE from a CNA (vendor-as-CNA, or MITRE/a national CERT as the root). Public disclosure
follows the coordinated timeline, not before. If the vendor refuses a CVE, escalate to the
national CERT (e.g.\ CISA, CERT/CC, BSI/CERT-Bund) which can act as a coordinating CNA and assign
one over the vendor's objection; you may then publish on the CERT's timeline.\\[2pt]
(3)~It is an \emph{n-day}: the fix exists publicly (in v1.5), so the vulnerability is known and a
patch is available -- by definition not a 0-day. For an asset owner this raises priority: an
n-day with a shipped fix means attackers can diff the patch and weaponise it quickly, so devices
still on v1.4 are at \emph{higher} near-term exploitation risk and the upgrade should be expedited
rather than deferred.\\[2pt]
\emph{Rubric hint:} full marks require the PSIRT-first-then-CERT escalation path and the
n-day/0-day distinction tied to ``patch exists $\Rightarrow$ patch-diffing accelerates attacks''.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 1.8 -- Coordinated-disclosure email (model answer).}\\[2pt]
\begin{lstlisting}
To: psirt@acme-industrial.example
Subject: [Security] Acme IG-200 v3.1 - unauthenticated firmware update (CRC-only)

Hello Acme PSIRT,

I am a security researcher reporting a vulnerability found during authorised
analysis of firmware I am entitled to possess. No third-party or production
device was involved; my proof-of-concept is held privately.

Affected product : Acme IG-200 industrial gateway
Firmware version : v3.1
Image SHA-256    : 3f9a...c21e

Summary: apply_update() accepts a new image when a CRC-32 over the downloaded
file equals a value taken from the attacker-supplied update header. CRC-32 is
an integrity checksum, not an authenticity check, so a crafted image with a
matching CRC is flashed and executed. An adjacent unbounded sprintf into a
64-byte stack buffer is a secondary issue.

CWE: CWE-345/CWE-347 (improper verification of update authenticity);
     CWE-787/CWE-121 (stack buffer overflow).

Minimal reproduction (no exploit code): point the update URL at any image,
set the header CRC to that image's CRC-32; the device flashes it. Details and
PoC available privately on request.

Suggested remediation: verify an asymmetric signature (vendor public key in
the bootloader) before flash_write; enforce a monotonic version floor.

Proposed timeline: 90 days for vendor remediation, +90 for ICS rollout
(180 total), with national-CERT (CISA/CERT-Bund) coordination from day 0;
public advisory on patch availability or at day 180, whichever is first.

Contact: <name>, <email>; PGP fingerprint <ABCD 1234 ... 7890>.
Authorised use only - analysed on owned hardware; PoC remains private.

Regards, <name>
\end{lstlisting}
\emph{Rubric hint:} must contain product+version+hash, factual summary, minimal repro with
\emph{no} weaponised code, ICS-appropriate timeline (180-ish days, CERT in the loop), CWE,
remediation, contact + PGP, and an authorised-use line. Deduct for ultimatums, threats, exploit
code, or unsupported claims.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 1.9 -- Supply chain and SBOM (essay rubric).}\\[2pt]
\emph{Rubric (5~points).}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{What an SBOM enables} (1~pt): on CVE-day, the asset owner queries their SBOM
        inventory and immediately answers ``which of my devices contain Boa / CODESYS runtime
        version X?'' -- turning a multi-week per-vendor enquiry into a lookup, and driving
        prioritised mitigation. Without an SBOM that mapping is opaque.
  \item \textbf{Two hard limits} (2~pts): (i)~SBOMs commonly stop at the top dependency tier and
        miss \emph{transitive} or statically-linked components; (ii)~most shipped firmware
        carries \emph{no} SBOM at all, and a declared version can diverge from the actual binary
        (back-ported patches, vendor forks, dead code). Hence firmware RE remains necessary to
        confirm what is \emph{really} in the image -- the SBOM is a claim, the binary is ground
        truth.
  \item \textbf{Regulatory driver + a vendor obligation} (1~pt): US EO~14028 with the NTIA
        ``minimum elements'' for an SBOM, or the EU Cyber Resilience Act. A concrete vendor
        obligation: under the CRA, manufacturers must provide an SBOM, handle vulnerabilities
        across the support lifetime, and report actively-exploited vulnerabilities (to ENISA)
        within tight deadlines; under EO~14028, suppliers to the US government must provide an
        SBOM.
  \item \textbf{Writing, structure, no invented sources} (1~pt).
\end{enumerate}
A strong essay explicitly states that an SBOM shifts the asset owner from reactive
vendor-by-vendor enquiry to proactive inventory-driven response, while conceding that RE is the
verification backstop because SBOMs are self-declared and incomplete.\\[2pt]
\emph{Sources:} Microsoft Boa report, 2022; CISA/CODESYS \emph{Code16} advisories, 2023; US
EO~14028 (2021) + NTIA SBOM minimum elements; Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 (Cyber Resilience Act).
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{hintbox}
Across the sheet, the recurring distinction is \emph{integrity vs.\ authenticity}: a hash or CRC
proves bytes are unchanged; only a signature proves \emph{who} produced them. Most firmware
findings -- tampered mirrors (1.1), CRC-only updates (1.5), silent fixes (1.7) -- collapse onto
that one axis. State it explicitly wherever it applies.
\end{hintbox}
