\noindent\emph{Goal.} These exercises take you from a blank greenfield sheet to a defensible secure-by-design architecture. You will run the IEC~62443-3-2 zone/conduit workflow and set a target Security Level (SL-T) per zone, select Foundational and System Requirements at that level (62443-3-3), choose between a data diode and a firewall, draft procurement clauses against the component requirements (62443-4-2), design a brokered remote-access plane, keep the SIS independent of the BPCS (IEC~61511), and define the secure-development and patch lifecycle (62443-4-1 / 2-3). This is a \emph{design} sheet: you produce architecture artefacts and justifications, not exploits. It aligns with --- but does not duplicate --- the NorthCell lab; reuse a scenario of your own where one is not supplied. Cite real standard parts and clause families throughout. Each question is intended to be answered in 5--12 sentences or one compact table.

\begin{taskbox}{Exercise 5.1 -- Derive Zones, Conduits and SL-T for a Greenfield Process}
You are the lead security architect for the FEED phase of a greenfield specialty-chemicals batch plant. Nothing is installed yet, so whatever you specify becomes contractually binding. The process, top to bottom:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item a continuous reactor train under a BPCS: per-cell PLCs, drives, line controllers (Purdue L1--L2);
  \item a dedicated SIS that trips an exothermic reactor and a toxic-storage emergency vent (highest consequence: people);
  \item a supervisory layer: SCADA/HMI servers and a local historian collector (L2--L3);
  \item a manufacturing layer: MES and a batch-record / quality data lake (L3);
  \item an industrial DMZ brokering all OT$\leftrightarrow$IT traffic (L3.5);
  \item an enterprise layer: ERP, corporate IT, internet egress (L4--L5).
\end{itemize}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Partition the System under Consideration into zones. Justify each grouping by \emph{shared threat profile, worst-case consequence, and change-management cadence} --- not by where it sits on a diagram.
  \item Assign an SL-T (1--4) to every zone. Anchor each justification to the \emph{worst-case physical consequence}, the plausible threat actor, and feasibility on commodity equipment. State which zone (if any) you push to SL-T\,4 and which you deliberately cap at SL-T\,2.
  \item Enumerate every conduit that crosses a zone boundary: one row per conduit with name, source, destination, protocol, L4 port, direction, data sensitivity, and the SL-T the conduit must carry (a conduit inherits the higher SL-T of the two zones it joins).
\end{enumerate}
Drivers: the plant ships globally and is in NIS2 scope (\emph{Manufacturing}); the insurer requires an IEC~62443 conformance statement. Present the result as a zone table and a conduit table, and mark which conduits are data-diode candidates.
\end{taskbox}

\begin{hintbox}
SL-T scales with unmitigated risk (likelihood $\times$ consequence) measured against tolerable risk --- the \emph{gap} sets the target, not the network exposure. The only zones where an integrity attack moves atoms into a hazard are the safety and safety-adjacent control zones; those earn the highest SL-T. A high-exposure but low-consequence-if-contained zone (the iDMZ) inherits the SL-T of the highest zone it must protect.
\end{hintbox}

\begin{taskbox}{Exercise 5.2 -- Select FRs and SRs for One Zone at Its SL-T}
Take your highest-SL non-safety zone from Exercise~5.1 (typically the BPCS control zone at SL-T\,3). The seven Foundational Requirements (FR1~IAC, FR2~UC, FR3~SI, FR4~DC, FR5~RDF, FR6~TRE, FR7~RA) apply to every zone; the SL ladder scales the rigor and is cumulative (SL3~$\supseteq$~SL2~$\supseteq$~SL1).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For this zone, name the specific System Requirements (SRs) from IEC~62443-3-3 that the SL-T pulls in --- at least one SR per FR --- and write in one sentence what each means \emph{at this plant}. Worked example to extend: FR1 / SR~1.1--1.2 (per-user accounts, no shared logins), FR3 / SR~3.1, 3.4 (communication and firmware integrity), FR7 / SR~7.3--7.4 (backup and verified restore of control logic).
  \item Identify one FR you would deliberately set \emph{below} the zone SL-T (e.g.\ FR4 confidentiality on a cyclic field bus where encryption adds intolerable jitter). Justify the documented downgrade, and name the FR you compensate at instead. State why an \emph{undocumented} downgrade is an audit finding while a documented one is good engineering.
\end{enumerate}
\end{taskbox}

\begin{taskbox}{Exercise 5.3 -- Data Diode vs.\ Firewall: Place and Justify}
You have three candidate boundaries:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item SIS / safety telemetry $\to$ historian;
  \item historian mirror $\to$ corporate analytics in the iDMZ;
  \item remote-vendor support $\to$ a machine cell.
\end{enumerate}
For each boundary, decide whether a \textbf{data diode} (hardware-enforced one-way), a \textbf{stateful firewall with a deny-by-default allow-list}, or both in series is the correct control. For each:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item justify the choice from the directionality of the \emph{genuine} data requirement;
  \item name one failure mode of the \emph{wrong} choice (e.g.\ a diode someone ``works around'' with a side channel; a firewall mis-rule that opens a return path into the SIS);
  \item state the residual risk you accept.
\end{itemize}
\end{taskbox}

\begin{taskbox}{Exercise 5.4 -- Three Procurement Security Clauses (IEC 62443-4-2)}
For a PLC destined for an SL-T\,3 control zone, write \textbf{three} contract-ready procurement security clauses. Each clause must:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item be specific enough to test on arrival (an inspector can produce a bench pass/fail);
  \item cite the relevant 62443-4-2 Component Requirement family (e.g.\ CR~1.x IAC, CR~2.x UC, CR~3.x SI, CR~7.x RA);
  \item state the required component capability security level (SL-C).
\end{enumerate}
Cover at minimum: individual accounts with no hard-coded credentials; signed-firmware and signed-logic integrity verified on load; and backup/restore to a known-good state. Then add:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item one cross-reference to a 62443-4-1 lifecycle obligation (SBOM format and cadence, vulnerability-handling SLA, or support window);
  \item one acceptance hook (rejection on arrival if any clause fails the ATP).
\end{itemize}
\end{taskbox}

\begin{taskbox}{Exercise 5.5 -- Brokered Remote-Access Pattern (PAM / Jump Host)}
Fourteen OEMs need remote support for the plant's 20-year life. Design a single identity-brokered remote-access pattern that lives in the iDMZ and is the \emph{only} way any external party touches OT.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Draw or describe the flow: vendor $\to$ internet $\to$ SSO+MFA portal $\to$ policy/PAM broker $\to$ time-boxed jump host $\to$ target asset. State explicitly how a vendor credential is prevented from ever existing on an OT asset.
  \item List \textbf{everything the pattern must log} for the session to count as an audit and forensic artefact: the authenticated identity and OEM, the target asset and its micro-zone, the start/stop timestamps, the approving operator, the commands and keystrokes, the screen recording, files transferred in and out, and the broker-issued credential ID.
  \item Add operator-in-the-loop approval, per-vendor least privilege (each OEM reaches only its own equipment's micro-zone), and a dual-control break-glass path for when the broker itself is down.
  \item Name the anti-pattern this design kills, and the residual risk it introduces (hint: the broker becomes a crown-jewel asset).
\end{enumerate}
\end{taskbox}

\begin{taskbox}{Exercise 5.6 -- Keep the SIS Independent of the BPCS (IEC 61511): Spot the Anti-Pattern}
An integrator submits a design in which a single engineering workstation can download both BPCS logic and SIS logic, and one combined HMI can both \emph{view} and \emph{write} safety bypasses, on a shared L2 control network.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Explain precisely why this couples safety to the most-exposed BPCS node and what IEC~61511 separation principle it violates. Connect it to the LOPA independence rule: why can a layer that shares the BPCS engineering workstation not be credited as an independent protection layer?
  \item Redesign it. State the independence measures:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
    \item separate logic solvers, separate I/O, and separate power from the BPCS;
    \item a view-only HMI path --- the shared HMI may read SIS state but never write a safety parameter or bypass;
    \item a single one-way, diode-protected telemetry egress, with \emph{no} inbound conduit from the BPCS, the iDMZ, or the remote-access broker into the SIS zone;
    \item overrides/forces gated by local, key-switched, dual authorisation that raises an alarm and can never be set remotely.
  \end{itemize}
  \item Reference the co-engineering view (IEC~TR~63069) and say what a cyber-security requirements specification contributes to the 61511 safety requirements specification. What does engineering the worst consequence out (a hardwired, non-programmable trip) add beyond ``more controls''?
\end{enumerate}
\end{taskbox}

\begin{taskbox}{Exercise 5.7 -- Secure-Development and Patch-Management Lifecycle (4-1 / 2-3)}
A perfect day-one design decays without a lifecycle. Define two linked processes and how they connect.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Vendor SDL gate (IEC~62443-4-1).} What every supplier must evidence before and during the contract: SDL conformance, an SBOM per release, a product threat model reviewed each major release, a vulnerability-handling SLA per ISO/IEC~30111, and the on-arrival ATP from Exercise~5.4.
  \item \emph{Asset-owner patch programme (IEC~62443-2-3 + 2-1).} The staged pipeline: vendor advisory $\to$ risk triage against the zone model $\to$ test on an offline twin of the affected cell $\to$ scheduled maintenance window $\to$ documented back-out plan written \emph{before} touching the running plant. State the decision rule for \emph{not} patching, and why OT patch decisions are risk decisions signed off with operations and safety, not an IT ``patch Tuesday''.
  \item \emph{Backups and recovery.} Specify the immutable-backup triad (production / off-site / WORM) with a quarterly tested restore, and name what is in scope (control logic, HMI projects, historian, firewall/switch configs).
\end{enumerate}
\end{taskbox}

\begin{taskbox}{Exercise 5.8 -- Brownfield vs.\ Greenfield Trade-off (Essay)}
In four to six paragraphs, argue when a brownfield compensating-controls strategy is the right call and when a greenfield rebuild is. Address all of:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item the cost asymmetry: a design-phase decision vs.\ a 5--10$\times$ retrofit during a planned outage;
  \item what you can and cannot achieve with wrapper appliances and network-layer compensations around a 25-year-old PLC whose vendor is gone;
  \item the sequencing constraint: you cannot segment what you have not inventoried, nor retire what you have not replaced;
  \item how you frame the residual-risk acceptance to safety, operations, and finance, with a named owner and a review date.
\end{itemize}
Conclude with a one-sentence decision rule a programme lead could apply.
\end{taskbox}

\begin{taskbox}{Exercise 5.9 -- STRIDE Your Own Architecture (Stretch)}
\emph{Stretch / optional.} Attack your own design from the inside.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For the three highest-SL conduits from Exercise~5.1, build a STRIDE-per-conduit table (Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege).
  \item For each applicable threat, name the control in your design that addresses it and the FR it maps to.
  \item Any threat with no mapped control is a gap --- list the gaps separately rather than hiding them in the table.
  \item State, in INL CCE terms, the one consequence you would \emph{engineer out} so that no cyber path can reach it (e.g.\ a hardwired, non-programmable trip that a fully compromised BPCS still cannot defeat).
\end{enumerate}
\end{taskbox}
