\noindent\textbf{Note to graders.}
These answers are at byte-level depth on purpose. Full credit requires the
student to name the concrete field, offset, or value -- not a paraphrase.
Equivalent encodings or tool output (Wireshark field names) are acceptable
where they identify the same construct. ``Add authentication'' or ``use a
firewall'' without a named mechanism is never full credit.

\vspace{0.4em}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 3.1 -- S7commPlus Anti-Replay/Integrity Field.}
(1) Offset~0 is the S7commPlus \emph{protocol id} \texttt{0x72} (the magic
that discriminates S7P from classic S7comm). Offset~1 is the \emph{protocol
version}: here \texttt{0x03}, which is the integrity-protected variant
shipped on S7-1500 / S7-1200 v4+. Values \texttt{0x01}/\texttt{0x02} are the
earlier reproducible-KDF variants without the trailing digest; the presence
of \texttt{0x03} plus the trailing integrity TLV proves v3.
(2) Bytes at offsets~8--9 (\texttt{ec a5}) are the per-session
\emph{sequence number} (a.k.a.\ integrity-id / message counter) that
increments by one per data PDU -- this is the \textbf{anti-replay} field. The
block after \texttt{a1 00} is the integrity TLV: \texttt{a1} = integrity
part tag, then a digest of length \texttt{0x1f}/\texttt{0x20} bytes -- a
keyed HMAC (SHA-256 family) computed over the PDU body and the counter using
the session key -- this is the \textbf{integrity} primitive. They combine
because the digest covers the counter, so a replayed body fails the check
once the counter has moved on: integrity binds the frame, the counter binds
it to a position in the session.
(3) \emph{Weakness:} the session key derives from the handshake challenges
through a fixed, publicly reverse-engineered KDF (Lei/He et al.\
2017--2020). An on-path attacker who captured the handshake can re-derive
the key and therefore recompute a valid digest for any counter value -- so
for the v1/v2 variants, and for v3 against an attacker who saw the
challenge exchange, the integrity tag is forgeable: it is obfuscation, not
authenticated encryption against a passive-then-active adversary. What it
\emph{still} stops: a blind off-path attacker who never saw the handshake
cannot produce a valid digest, and a naive replay of a captured PDU fails
because its stale counter no longer matches the live session.
\textit{Rubric: $-1$ each for misnaming \texttt{0x72}/\texttt{0x03}; full
credit on (3) needs the ``reproducible KDF $\Rightarrow$ forgeable digest''
chain, not just ``it is weak''.}
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 3.2 -- GOOSE Spoofing Window.}
(1) The subscriber accepts any frame whose \texttt{stNum} exceeds the last
seen, immediately and with no nonce or signature. So the moment the attacker
sends \texttt{stNum = 8}, it is accepted. The \emph{window} is the interval
in which $8$ is still the highest number the subscriber has seen -- i.e.\
from now until the \emph{legitimate} publisher next increments its own
\texttt{stNum} (on its next real event) to $8$ or beyond, at which point the
attacker's frame is no longer ``newer''. In steady state the publisher only
bumps \texttt{stNum} on an actual data change, so at the heartbeat cadence
($\texttt{maxTime}=1000$\,ms) the window is effectively open until the next
genuine event. Concretely: the attacker's forged state survives until either
(a) the real publisher issues \texttt{stNum} $\ge 9$, or (b) the
\texttt{timeAllowedtoLive} of the last \emph{genuine} frame expires and the
subscriber flags loss-of-comms -- whichever comes first. With
$\texttt{TAL}=1000$\,ms the subscriber will not even declare staleness for
$\sim$1\,s, so a single spoof at \texttt{stNum=8} typically dominates for
the better part of a heartbeat period. (Numerically: window
$\approx \min(\,t_{\text{next genuine event}},\ \texttt{TAL})$; here the
guaranteed-accepted floor is the instant of injection and the practical
ceiling is $1000$\,ms.)
(2) Three field edits (TLV bytes):
\texttt{85 01 07}~$\to$~\texttt{85 01 08} (\texttt{stNum}: $7\to8$);
\texttt{86 01 00} stays \texttt{86 01 00} (reset \texttt{sqNum}~$=0$ for a
new state -- if it was nonzero, force it to \texttt{00}); and flip the
boolean data member in \texttt{allData} from \texttt{83 01 00} (FALSE) to
\texttt{83 01 ff} (TRUE) to assert the trip. The attacker must also keep
\texttt{gocbRef}/\texttt{datSet}/\texttt{goID}/\texttt{appID} identical so
the subscriber maps it to the same control block.
(3) \emph{R-GOOSE} (routable GOOSE, IEC 61850-90-5) adds a session/SPDU
wrapper with a signature/HMAC and a monotonic session counter so the frame
can cross L3 and be authenticated; objection: the added header plus crypto
processing and the UDP/IP path raise end-to-end latency, threatening the
sub-4\,ms transfer-trip budget. \emph{IEC 62351-6} adds an authentication
tag (HMAC-SHA256, or signature) appended to the GOOSE PDU; objection: the
same -- per-frame MAC computation on every IED at the 4\,ms cadence is what
protection engineers say busts the latency budget on legacy CPUs, which is
why adoption in production substations is still rare.
\textit{Rubric: the window argument must tie to ``higher \texttt{stNum}
accepted instantly'' AND to TAL/heartbeat bounding it; full credit on (3)
needs the latency objection for each, not just naming the standards.}
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 3.3 -- IEC 61850 MMS Report-Control Structure.}
(1) \texttt{RptID} \texttt{"LD0/LLN0\$RP\$urcb01"} identifies the report and,
by the ObjectReference path, the logical device LD0, logical node LLN0, the
\texttt{RP} (report) functional constraint, and the control block instance
\texttt{urcb01}. \texttt{SqNum = 42} (\texttt{85 04 00 00 00 2a}) is the
report's own sequence number, used by the client to detect lost/duplicated
reports. \texttt{ConfRev = 1} (\texttt{86 04 00 00 00 01}) is the
configuration revision of the data set -- if it does not match what the
client expects, the data-set membership changed and the report layout is no
longer trusted. The \texttt{a2 ...} \texttt{SEQUENCE OF AccessResult} is the
actual payload: \texttt{83 01 ff} is \texttt{BOOLEAN = TRUE} -- e.g.\ a
breaker/position/alarm point now asserted; the \texttt{BITSTRING} is the
associated IEC 61850 \emph{quality} (validity/source bits) and the
\texttt{UtcTime} is the timestamp of the change.
(2) A \textbf{URCB} (unbuffered report-control block) sends reports only when
a client is connected and does not retain events across a connection loss; a
\textbf{BRCB} (buffered) queues events with an \texttt{EntryID} so they
survive a disconnect and are replayed on reconnect. This is a \emph{URCB}:
the name is \texttt{urcb01} and there is no \texttt{EntryID}/\texttt{TimeOfEntry}
member in the structure (those appear only in buffered reports).
(3) In the default MMS profile \emph{nothing} authenticates the report --
MMS over ISO-on-TCP (RFC 1006) carries no message authentication. A host on
the segment can therefore replay a captured \texttt{informationReport} (feed
the HMI a stale boolean and timestamp) or forge one (assert a false breaker
state). IEC 62351-4 wraps the MMS association in TLS (mutual X.509),
providing transport-level confidentiality, integrity, and peer
authentication, which defeats both replay and forgery -- but only between
endpoints that both implement it.
\textit{Rubric: full credit needs URCB-vs-BRCB justified by name + missing
EntryID, and the 62351-4 = TLS answer. Accept ``RptID is an
ObjectReference'' phrasing.}
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 3.4 -- DLMS/COSEM Access-Control Reasoning.}
(1) The \texttt{mechanism-name} is the COSEM security mechanism OID
\texttt{2.16.756.5.8.2.\textit{n}} (encoded \texttt{60 85 74 05 08 02
0x}); the tail \texttt{...02.03} selects mechanism~\texttt{3}, which is
\textbf{HLS (High Level Security) with GMAC} -- DLMS authentication
\emph{level 3 (high-GMAC)}. Mechanism~0 = none (level~0), 1 = LLS / shared
password (level~1), 2 = HLS-MD5, 3 = HLS-GMAC; the \texttt{.03} suffix is
the discriminator.
(2) It is a mutual challenge-response. The client sends an 8-byte CtoS
(client-to-server) challenge in \texttt{calling-authentication-value}; the
meter must return a value derived from CtoS under the shared key (proving it
holds the key and binding to \emph{this} fresh challenge), and the meter
sends its own StoC, which the client answers in the next APDU. Because each
side's response is keyed to the \emph{other side's} random challenge,
recording one full AARQ/AARE pair lets the attacker replay only that exact
exchange against a party that happens to reissue the identical challenge --
which a correct implementation never does (challenges are random/nonce-like).
The attacker cannot derive the key, cannot answer a \emph{new} challenge,
and so cannot open a fresh authenticated association or forge subsequent
GMAC-protected APDUs.
(3) BSI TR-03109 mandates the German \emph{Smart Meter Gateway} (SMGW) as the
security anchor: meter-to-SMGW and SMGW-to-HES communication is protected
with X.509 certificates and AES-GCM (TLS to the WAN, end-to-end content
encryption to market participants), i.e.\ a high, certificate-backed level
-- \emph{not} a meter-local shared password. Low-level (LLS, level~1)
association is forbidden because a single shared password gives no per-device
identity, no replay protection, no integrity, and is trivially harvested
from one compromised meter to impersonate the fleet.
\textit{Rubric: full credit needs ``mechanism 3 = HLS-GMAC'' read from the
OID tail, the mutual-nonce replay argument, and TR-03109 = SMGW + X.509 +
AES-GCM. Accept ``GCM/GMAC'' interchangeably for the primitive.}
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 3.5 -- Identify the Protocol and Function.}
\textbf{A:} \texttt{68 .. 68 .. 16} fixed-length framing with two
\texttt{0x68} start bytes and a \texttt{0x16} stop byte is
\textbf{IEC 60870-5-101/104} (here the FT1.2 / fixed style; the \texttt{68}
sentinels and \texttt{16} terminator are the family signature). The type
identifier \texttt{0x65} = \texttt{101} decimal is \emph{C\_RD\_NA\_1 / clock
or interrogation}-class control depending on dataset; more generally the TI
octet after the APCI is what selects the ASDU service -- a
\texttt{0x46}/\texttt{0x2e}-class TI with a write/command gates a control
action. The field that gates a state change is the \emph{cause-of-transmission}
plus the command TI (e.g.\ C\_SC\_NA\_1 single command).
\textbf{B:} \texttt{05 64} is the \textbf{DNP3} data-link start. After it the
length octet \texttt{0x0b} (11) and the control octet \texttt{0x44} encode
the frame: bit~7 (DIR) and bit~6 (PRM) of \texttt{0x44} = \texttt{0100 0100}
$\Rightarrow$ DIR=0, PRM=1 (primary, master-to-outstation direction in this
encoding), function code in the low nibble \texttt{0x4} = \emph{unconfirmed
user data}. Destination \texttt{0a 00}=10, source \texttt{01 00}=1. The
application function (after the transport byte \texttt{c0} and app control
\texttt{c1}) \texttt{81} would be unsolicited; \texttt{c0 c1 81}
here is the app layer -- the function code byte is what gates a write/operate
(e.g.\ \texttt{0x05} OPERATE).
\textbf{C:} \texttt{03 00 00 16} is \textbf{TPKT} (version~3, length~0x16=22).
The next layer \texttt{11 e0} is COTP: length \texttt{0x11}, PDU type
\texttt{0xe0} = \emph{CR, Connection Request}. This is the COTP connection
phase; the parameters \texttt{c1 02 01 00} (calling TSAP) and
\texttt{c2 02 01 02} (called TSAP) negotiate the source/destination TSAPs
(rack/slot for S7), and \texttt{c0 01 0a} sets TPDU size $2^{10}$. No
read/write is gated yet -- this is association setup that precedes any S7 job.
\textit{Rubric: protocol family + the gating field per payload. Accept
``IEC 60870-5'' for A without the exact TI if the \texttt{68/16} framing and
TI-selects-service point is made; B must get DNP3 + DIR/PRM read from
\texttt{0x44}; C must get TPKT+COTP CR.}
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 3.6 -- Passive Detection Rule (model: GOOSE high-stNum).}
(1) Invariant: for a given (\texttt{appID}, \texttt{goID},
\texttt{gocbRef}), there is exactly \emph{one} legitimate publisher (one
source MAC) and \texttt{stNum} is monotonic non-decreasing with bounded
increments; \texttt{sqNum} resets to 0 only when \texttt{stNum} increments;
the inter-frame gap follows the published retransmission schedule
(\texttt{minTime} doubling to \texttt{maxTime}).
(2) Zeek-style stateful logic:
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
state[goID].pub_mac, state[goID].max_stNum
on each GOOSE frame:
  if src_mac != state[goID].pub_mac:        -> ALERT "GOOSE publisher change"
  if stNum > state[goID].max_stNum + 1:     -> ALERT "stNum jump"
  if stNum == state[goID].max_stNum and sqNum < last_sqNum:
                                            -> ALERT "sqNum regression"
  if delta_t << minTime (two frames same stNum too close):
                                            -> ALERT "injection burst"
  update state[goID]
\end{lstlisting}
The fields keyed are \texttt{goID}/\texttt{appID} (identity), source MAC
(publisher binding), and the (\texttt{stNum}, \texttt{sqNum}) pair plus
arrival time (the stateful condition). The rule fires when a second source
asserts a higher \texttt{stNum} for an existing control block.
(3) False positive: a legitimate publisher restart or IED failover changes
the source MAC and may jump \texttt{stNum}, tripping the publisher-change /
jump alerts. Evasion: an attacker who first \emph{silences} the real
publisher (e.g.\ ARP/L2 disruption or unplugging) and then takes over its
exact source MAC with \texttt{stNum = max+1} produces a stream
indistinguishable from a clean failover -- the rule cannot tell a forged
takeover from a real one without a cryptographic binding (i.e.\ 62351-6).
\textit{Rubric: accept S7P-replay (key on integrity-id monotonicity per TCP
session) or MMS-replay (key on report \texttt{SqNum}/\texttt{ConfRev}
monotonicity) instead. Full credit needs invariant + concrete keyed field +
one FP and one evasion.}
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 3.7 -- Authentication Across Four Stacks.}
\begin{center}\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\par\begin{tabular}{p{2.3cm}p{2.6cm}p{2.6cm}p{2.6cm}p{2.7cm}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{S7commPlus v3} & \textbf{GOOSE (62351-6)} & \textbf{DNP3-SAv5} & \textbf{OPC UA Basic256Sha256} \\
\midrule
Auth mech.\ & session key from KDF over handshake & HMAC/sig tag on PDU & pre-shared update key, HMAC challenge & X.509 mutual + user token \\
Anti-replay & per-session counter (integrity-id) & \texttt{stNum}/\texttt{sqNum} (+62351 ctr) & CSQ \& challenge nonce & sequence \# + session nonce \\
Integrity & keyed digest (HMAC-SHA family) & HMAC-SHA256 & HMAC-SHA-256 (SAv5) & RSA-SHA256 signature \\
Confident.\ & none (payload not encrypted) & none (signed, not encrypted) & none (auth only) & AES-256-CBC (SignAndEncrypt) \\
Default-on? & yes (v3 firmware) & no (rarely enabled) & no (often disabled) & no (config can be None) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent The strongest \emph{default} guarantee in practice is S7commPlus
v3: on current firmware the integrity counter and keyed digest are on
without operator action, so an unconfigured device still resists blind
replay -- whereas GOOSE 62351-6, DNP3-SAv5 and OPC UA all default to a weak
or null mode (no auth, SA disabled, \texttt{SecurityPolicy = None}) and only
become secure once an engineer correctly configures them. That is why
``default-on'' matters more than peak capability in OT: brownfield devices
are commissioned once, by integrators under time pressure, and the
as-found configuration is what an attacker meets years later. OPC UA has the
strongest \emph{ceiling} (mutual X.509 + AES-GCM), but its repeatedly
observed real-world default of \texttt{None} means the ceiling is irrelevant
to the median deployment. The lesson: evaluate the shipped default and the
likelihood it is changed, not the spec's best mode.
\textit{Rubric: cell mechanisms may use equivalent names; the paragraph must
argue default-on $>$ peak capability and cite the OPC~UA \texttt{None}
default as the counter-example.}
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 3.8 -- Essay: Bump-in-the-Wire Crypto (model points).}
A full-credit essay covers, with concrete grounding:
\emph{Installed base / firmware freeze:} brownfield plants run IEDs and PLCs
that are out of vendor support or validated against a frozen firmware; an
in-protocol crypto upgrade requires a firmware change on every device, which
many cannot accept. BITW adds security \emph{outside} the device, leaving
the validated firmware untouched.
\emph{Safety re-certification cost:} touching IED firmware in a
safety-relevant loop (SIS, protection) can invalidate the functional-safety
case (IEC 61508/61511) and force re-validation; an inline gateway that does
not alter the control logic is far cheaper to qualify.
\emph{Latency budget:} GOOSE/SV transfer-trip lives in a sub-4\,ms budget;
a BITW MAC/encrypt step adds latency, so for those flows BITW often cannot
sit in-line and L2 isolation or 62351-6 is used instead -- but for slower
flows (MMS, S7, DNP3, historian links) a TLS/IPsec gateway fits comfortably.
A strong answer states \emph{where} BITW can and cannot sit.
\emph{Key management at scale:} BITW concentrates keys/certs at a manageable
number of gateways rather than thousands of heterogeneous devices, so PKI
lifecycle (issuance, rotation, CRL/OCSP) is tractable; in-protocol crypto
pushes that burden onto every device with its own vendor tooling.
\emph{Residual risk:} the segment \emph{behind} the gateway is still
plaintext and unauthenticated, so a foothold inside the protected zone (a
compromised engineering laptop on the same VLAN) bypasses BITW entirely --
BITW protects the conduit, not the zone.
Cite at least two of: IEC 62351 (the in-protocol comparison point),
MACsec/IEEE 802.1AE (L2 BITW), an OT VPN/IPsec gateway or data diode, and
the brownfield reality that in-protocol upgrades stall on vendor support
matrices.
\textit{Rubric (out of 5): 1 pt installed-base/firmware-freeze; 1 pt
safety re-cert; 1 pt latency placement (where BITW can/can't sit); 1 pt key
mgmt at fleet scale; 1 pt residual-plaintext-zone risk. Deduct for no named
standard/product and for treating BITW as a complete fix.}
\end{solutionbox}
