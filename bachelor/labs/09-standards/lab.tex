\section*{Background -- what is a zone-and-conduit risk assessment?}

IEC 62443-3-2 (\emph{Security risk assessment for system design}) defines a seven-step workflow called the \emph{Zone and Conduit Requirements (ZCR)}. The mental model is the same as a fire-safety review: split the plant into compartments (\emph{zones}) joined by clearly named pipes (\emph{conduits}); rate the consequence and likelihood of an incident in each compartment; assign a target rigor (\emph{Security Level Target, SL-T}, 1--4); map that rigor to specific engineering duties (\emph{Foundational Requirements}, FR\,1--7) drawn from IEC 62443-3-3.

You do not need any electrical-engineering background to do this. Everything below is networking, system administration, and structured writing. The lab plant is the same Docker stack you have used since Lab 01; treat it as a \emph{very small} manufacturing-of-motor-vehicles cell (a single press, one HMI, one engineering workstation, one IIoT broker).

\section*{ZCR 2 -- Partition the SUC into zones}

\begin{stepbox}{2 -- Define at least three zones}
A \emph{zone} is a set of assets that share the same security requirements -- same threat profile, same consequence-of-failure, same controls. Draw your zone boundaries with these defaults; justify each in one sentence:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Field Zone (Z-FIELD).} \texttt{openplc} + \texttt{opcua} + \texttt{zeek}. Justification: real-time process control; loss of integrity moves real material; same physical operator group; same SL-T candidate (high).
  \item \textbf{Operations Zone (Z-OPS).} \texttt{student} + \texttt{mqtt}. Justification: engineering workstation and IIoT broker; humans-in-the-loop; different change-management cadence than the PLC; needs broad outbound reach for tools.
  \item \textbf{Engineering / DMZ Zone (Z-DMZ).} \texttt{landing}. Justification: read-only documentation surface; the only zone reachable from the host (\texttt{127.0.0.1:8000}); lowest consequence, highest exposure.
\end{itemize}
Optionally split the Field Zone into \emph{Z-FIELD-CTRL} (openplc) and \emph{Z-FIELD-SCADA} (opcua) if you want to model a Level\,1 / Level\,2 split. Write your final zone list as a numbered set.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}{2b -- Sketch the zone drawing}
On paper, in \texttt{draw.io}, or in \texttt{excalidraw}: draw one box per zone. Inside each box, list the containers. Annotate each box with the Purdue level(s) it covers. Leave room around the boxes -- ZCR\,3 will add the conduits.
\end{stepbox}

\section*{ZCR 3 -- Identify the conduits}

A \emph{conduit} is any communication path that crosses a zone boundary. Every conduit needs a name, the two zones it connects, the protocol, the L4 port, the direction, and the data sensitivity.

\begin{stepbox}{3 -- Enumerate active flows}
Use the \texttt{student} container -- it sits in \texttt{Z-OPS} and can reach every other zone. First, a port sweep:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
docker compose exec student nmap -sT -p- --open -T4 172.28.0.5,10,11,12
\end{lstlisting}
Then a short passive capture to catch any background chatter the sweep would not provoke:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
docker compose exec -d student tcpdump -i eth0 -w /labs/captures/lab09-conduits.pcap \
  'net 172.28.0.0/16 and not arp'
sleep 30
docker compose exec student pkill -INT tcpdump
docker compose exec student tshark -r /labs/captures/lab09-conduits.pcap \
  -q -z conv,tcp 2>/dev/null | head -40
\end{lstlisting}
If you kept the PCAPs from Lab\,03 (the nmap sweeps) or Lab\,04 (the Modbus + OPC UA captures), reuse them -- this is exactly how a real auditor stitches evidence together across an engagement.

\section*{ZCR 4 -- Score unmitigated risk per conduit}

\begin{stepbox}{4 -- Likelihood $\times$ consequence, no controls assumed}
Pretend every control you can see in the stack (the Docker bridge isolation, the OpenPLC login, Zeek) does \emph{not} exist. For each conduit, score \emph{likelihood} (L) and \emph{consequence} (C) on a 1--5 scale, then compute risk $R = L \times C$. Use this rubric:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Likelihood.} 1\,=\,requires nation-state proximity; 3\,=\,a competent intern with internet access; 5\,=\,already happens daily by accident.
  \item \textbf{Consequence.} 1\,=\,nuisance; 3\,=\,a shift of lost production; 5\,=\,product damage, safety incident, or a regulator-visible event.
\end{itemize}

\section*{ZCR 5 -- Assign SL-T per zone}

\begin{stepbox}{5 -- Pick the target Security Level}
IEC 62443-3-3 defines four Security Levels:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{SL\,1} -- protection against casual or coincidental violation.
  \item \textbf{SL\,2} -- protection against intentional violation with low resources, generic skills, low motivation.
  \item \textbf{SL\,3} -- protection against intentional violation with moderate resources, ICS-specific skills, moderate motivation.
  \item \textbf{SL\,4} -- protection against intentional violation with extensive resources, ICS-specific skills, high motivation (nation-state).
\end{itemize}
Pick a target SL for each zone and write a one-sentence justification. The recommended assignment for this stack is:
\medskip

\noindent\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.20\textwidth} c p{0.55\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Zone} & \textbf{SL-T} & \textbf{Justification} \\
\midrule
Z-FIELD & SL-T\,3 & Worst-case consequence (product damage, safety) is high; the press is the only path to physical harm. A determined ICS-literate attacker (SL\,3 model) is realistic for a manufacturing site that ships globally. \\
Z-OPS   & SL-T\,2 & Engineering and IIoT functions; consequence is operational not safety; threat actor is a phisher or commodity malware (SL\,2). \\
Z-DMZ   & SL-T\,1 & Read-only public-ish surface; consequence of compromise is defacement of a documentation page. SL\,1 (accidental misuse) is sufficient -- raising it does not buy anything proportionate. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\medskip

\emph{Why is Z-FIELD higher than Z-DMZ?} Because the field zone is the only path through which an attacker can move \emph{atoms}. The DMZ is bytes. SL-T scales with worst-case physical consequence, not with packet volume or with how interesting the zone looks on a network diagram.
\end{stepbox}

\section*{ZCR 6 -- Map IEC 62443-3-3 Foundational Requirements to each zone}

\begin{stepbox}{6 -- Which FRs apply, at what SL?}
IEC 62443-3-3 defines seven Foundational Requirements:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{FR\,1} -- Identification \& Authentication Control (IAC)
  \item \textbf{FR\,2} -- Use Control (UC)
  \item \textbf{FR\,3} -- System Integrity (SI)
  \item \textbf{FR\,4} -- Data Confidentiality (DC)
  \item \textbf{FR\,5} -- Restricted Data Flow (RDF)
  \item \textbf{FR\,6} -- Timely Response to Events (TRE)
  \item \textbf{FR\,7} -- Resource Availability (RA)
\end{enumerate}
For each zone, write down which FRs must be satisfied and at what SL. \emph{All seven apply to every zone}; the SL ladder is how you scale the rigor. A defensible baseline:
\medskip

\noindent\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.18\textwidth} c c c c c c c@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Zone (SL-T)} & \textbf{FR1} & \textbf{FR2} & \textbf{FR3} & \textbf{FR4} & \textbf{FR5} & \textbf{FR6} & \textbf{FR7} \\
\midrule
Z-FIELD (SL\,3) & 3 & 3 & 3 & 2 & 3 & 3 & 3 \\
Z-OPS   (SL\,2) & 2 & 2 & 2 & 2 & 2 & 2 & 2 \\
Z-DMZ   (SL\,1) & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\medskip

For Z-FIELD, write a one-line gloss per FR -- this turns the table into a control specification:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{FR\,1 (IAC) @ SL\,3} -- per-user accounts, no shared service accounts, MFA at least for engineering changes.
  \item \textbf{FR\,2 (UC) @ SL\,3} -- role-based authorisation; engineers can download programs, operators only acknowledge alarms.
  \item \textbf{FR\,3 (SI) @ SL\,3} -- signed or hashed firmware; alarm on any unauthorised PLC program change.
  \item \textbf{FR\,4 (DC) @ SL\,2} -- field protocols rarely need confidentiality; downgrade is intentional, document it.
  \item \textbf{FR\,5 (RDF) @ SL\,3} -- enforced segmentation; only the listed conduits exist, and each is filtered.
  \item \textbf{FR\,6 (TRE) @ SL\,3} -- monitored 24/7; SOC playbook for Modbus anomalies; mean-time-to-detect documented.
  \item \textbf{FR\,7 (RA) @ SL\,3} -- redundancy or hot-standby for the PLC; DoS resistance verified.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Deliverable for this step:} the FR-per-zone table plus the one-line gloss for the highest-SL zone.
\end{stepbox}

\section*{Cross-reference -- NIS2 Annex II}

\begin{stepbox}{8 -- Does NIS2 apply if the lab plant were real?}
Treat the lab plant as a \emph{manufacturing of motor vehicles} cell (Annex II, Sector 5: \emph{Manufacturing}, sub-sector \emph{Manufacture of motor vehicles, trailers and semi-trailers} -- NACE C29). Answer the following in your notebook, citing the article numbers from Directive (EU) 2022/2555:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Does the entity fall under NIS2 at all?} See Art.\,2 (scope) and Art.\,3 (essential / important entities). Annex II sectors map to \emph{important entities} unless they also meet essential-entity thresholds. The size-cap rule (Art.\,2(1)): medium-sized enterprises and larger (50+ employees or EUR~10\,M+ turnover) are in scope.
  \item \textbf{Which obligations bite first?} Art.\,21 (cybersecurity risk-management measures -- the ten-point catalogue including risk analysis, incident handling, supply-chain security, and use of cryptography) and Art.\,23 (incident reporting: early warning within 24\,h, incident notification within 72\,h, final report within one month).
  \item \textbf{Who is accountable?} Art.\,20 -- the management body approves the risk-management measures, oversees their implementation, and can be held liable for non-compliance.
  \item \textbf{Who supervises?} Art.\,32--34 -- competent authorities supervise important entities ex-post; for essential entities Art.\,32 supervision is also ex-ante.
  \item \textbf{Worst-case fine?} Art.\,34(5): up to EUR~7\,M or 1.4\% of global annual turnover for important entities; up to EUR~10\,M or 2\% for essential entities. (Convert any figure to EUR; do not use the euro sign in this lab text.)
\end{itemize}

Write a one-paragraph verdict: \emph{Yes / No, NIS2 applies because $\ldots$; the obligations that bite first are $\ldots$; the management body is the named accountable function.} If your fictional plant is below the size cap, it is out of scope -- but Art.\,2(2) lists exceptions (sole providers, cross-border services) you should mention.
\end{stepbox}
