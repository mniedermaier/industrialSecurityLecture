\labheader

\begin{goalbox}
Walk the OpenPLC conveyor through a full Cyber-PHA methodology end-to-end: bow-tie, Cyber-HAZOP, LOPA, risk register, response decision, and a stretch FAIR estimate. The plant is a toy, but the procedure is exactly what you would run on a refinery or water utility -- only the asset list is shorter.
\end{goalbox}

\section*{Why this lab exists}

In labs~01--06 you ran tools against the conveyor. Here you do no scanning at all: you sit with the ladder source on screen and produce the paperwork an auditor actually asks for. Risk management is a \emph{writing} exercise -- the artefacts you produce here are what regulators (NIS2, NIST 800-82~R3, IEC 62443-3-2) demand.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{IT analogy.} A Cyber-PHA is the OT cousin of an IT threat model. Where STRIDE asks ``what can go wrong with this data flow?'', a Cyber-PHA asks ``what can go wrong with this \emph{physical process}, and which of those failures can an attacker cause on purpose?'' The verbs (\emph{No}, \emph{More}, \emph{Less}, \emph{Reverse}, \emph{As Well As}) replace STRIDE's letters; the rhythm is the same.

\section*{Part B -- Bow-tie: define the top event}

\begin{stepbox}{B1 -- Pick a top event}
A bow-tie analysis sits the \emph{top event} -- the moment things go from ``everything fine'' to ``incident'' -- at the centre of the diagram. For a conveyor, choose one:

\medskip
\begin{tabular}{p{8mm}p{145mm}}
$\square$ & \textbf{TE-A:} Motor runs while a guard is open $\rightarrow$ pinch injury to a maintenance worker. \\
$\square$ & \textbf{TE-B:} Conveyor jams under load, motor keeps energising $\rightarrow$ belt overheats, smoulders, ignites stored cardboard $\rightarrow$ warehouse fire. \\
$\square$ & \textbf{TE-C:} Stop button presses ignored $\rightarrow$ unsafe run-on past shift end. \\
\end{tabular}

\medskip\noindent
We will use \textbf{TE-B} for the rest of the exercise. It is the most consequence-rich and the most cyber-relevant.
\end{stepbox}

\section*{Part C -- Cyber-HAZOP: walk the guide words}

\begin{stepbox}{C1 -- Apply the IEC 61882 guide words}
A HAZOP picks a \emph{parameter} (a signal, a flow, a setpoint) and walks five deviation verbs over it. The classic verbs are \textbf{No}, \textbf{More}, \textbf{Less}, \textbf{Reverse}, and \textbf{As Well As}. Cyber-HAZOP adds the qualifier ``\ldots caused by an attacker.''

Pick \textbf{Motor} (\%QX0.0) as your parameter. Complete the table.

\medskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{|p{16mm}|p{32mm}|p{46mm}|p{46mm}|}
\hline
\textbf{Guide word} & \textbf{Deviation} & \textbf{Cyber cause} & \textbf{Consequence} \\
\hline
No          & Motor never runs                & e.g.\ Stop bit pinned \texttt{FALSE} & line halted, lost shift \\
\hline
More        & Motor runs harder / longer      & & \\
\hline
Less        & Motor under-runs / chatters     & & \\
\hline
Reverse     & Motor runs backwards            & & \\
\hline
As Well As  & Motor runs \emph{and} something else (lamp off, alarm muted) & & \\
\hline
\end{tabular}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Note.} The conveyor.st program does not expose a direction bit, so ``Reverse'' is \emph{not credible} for this plant -- write ``N/A: hardware-fixed single direction'' and move on. Documenting a verb as \emph{not applicable} is a HAZOP output, not a failure of the method.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}{C2 -- Repeat for one input}
Now repeat the same five-row table for \textbf{Guard1} (\%IX0.2). For each verb, ask the security-flavoured question: \emph{what if an attacker caused Guard1 to deviate that way?}

\medskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{|p{16mm}|p{32mm}|p{46mm}|p{46mm}|}
\hline
\textbf{Guide word} & \textbf{Deviation on Guard1} & \textbf{Cyber cause} & \textbf{Consequence} \\
\hline
No          & & & \\
\hline
More        & (degenerate -- it is a Boolean) & N/A & N/A \\
\hline
Less        & (degenerate -- it is a Boolean) & N/A & N/A \\
\hline
Reverse     & Guard reads as opposite state & & \\
\hline
As Well As  & Guard reads OK \emph{and} attacker writes Motor=1 & & \\
\hline
\end{tabular}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Lesson.} Most conveyor signals are Boolean, so three of the five guide words collapse. On a real plant with analogue signals (flow rate, pressure, temperature, level) all five verbs are live and each row yields one or two attacker scenarios. The full HAZOP grows quickly.
\end{stepbox}

\section*{Part D -- LOPA: count the layers}

\begin{stepbox}{D1 -- Inventory the protection layers}
LOPA (Layer Of Protection Analysis, CCPS~2001) lists every \emph{independent} barrier between an initiating event and the top event, and assigns each a probability of failure on demand (PFD). For each row below, decide whether the layer exists on the conveyor plant, then decide whether \emph{a single cyber attacker} can defeat it. Tick the boxes.

\medskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{|p{38mm}|p{42mm}|p{18mm}|p{42mm}|}
\hline
\textbf{Layer} & \textbf{Realisation on conveyor} & \textbf{Exists?} & \textbf{Cyber-defeatable?} \\
\hline
Process design (inherent) & Motor sized so it stalls without damage & $\square$ Y $\square$ N & \\
\hline
BPCS (main controller)    & The ladder seal-in latch itself & $\square$ Y $\square$ N & \\
\hline
Alarms + operator action  & Indicator lamp on the panel & $\square$ Y $\square$ N & \\
\hline
SIS (independent safety)  & Separate hard-wired e-stop \emph{not} via PLC & $\square$ Y $\square$ N & \\
\hline
Physical protection       & Mechanical guard around pinch points & $\square$ Y $\square$ N & \\
\hline
Emergency response        & Operator with fire extinguisher & $\square$ Y $\square$ N & \\
\hline
\end{tabular}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Independence is the value.} The whole LOPA argument relies on layers being independent: a single failure (or attack) must not collapse two layers at once. On the toy conveyor, the BPCS \emph{and} the lamp \emph{and} (if it existed) a software-only e-stop are all the same PLC -- one cyber compromise removes all three.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}{D2 -- Score the residual}
Multiply the PFD of every layer you ticked as ``Exists~AND~not cyber-defeatable'' to get the residual frequency of the top event. Use $10^{-1}$ for an alarm/operator layer, $10^{-2}$ for a SIL-2 SIS, $10^{-3}$ for an inherently-safer design, $1$ (no credit) for any layer that a cyber attacker can defeat.

\medskip
Initiating event frequency (cyber attempt against an Internet-exposed OpenPLC):
$f_{\textit{IE}} \approx 1\ \text{per year}$ (this is generous; see Shodan).

\medskip
Residual frequency:
\[
f_{\textit{TE}} \;=\; f_{\textit{IE}} \times \prod_{\text{independent layers}} \text{PFD}_i
\]

Write your computed $f_{\textit{TE}}$ here: \underline{\hspace{60mm}} per year.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Sanity check.} If your number is $\geq 10^{-2}$ per year (i.e.\ once every century or more often) for a fire-class consequence, the plant is \emph{not} ALARP-compliant under UK HSE doctrine and a regulator would demand additional layers.
\end{stepbox}

\section*{Part G -- (Stretch) FAIR quantification}

\begin{stepbox}{G1 -- Estimate Loss Event Frequency}
FAIR (\emph{Factor Analysis of Information Risk}, O-RA C20A:2021) decomposes annual loss into Loss Event Frequency (LEF) times Loss Magnitude (LM). Pick numbers you can defend in one sentence each.

\medskip
\begin{tabular}{|p{60mm}|p{30mm}|p{55mm}|}
\hline
\textbf{Factor} & \textbf{Estimate} & \textbf{Basis (one sentence)} \\
\hline
Threat Event Frequency (attempts/yr) &  & e.g.\ Shodan-exposed OpenPLC \\
\hline
Vulnerability (P[success $\mid$ attempt]) &  & default creds, no patching \\
\hline
\textbf{LEF = TEF $\times$ Vuln} &  & \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}{G2 -- Estimate Loss Magnitude}
Build LM bottom-up. Use \textbf{EUR}, not other currencies, and keep figures one significant digit. Do \emph{not} include human-life numbers -- see warnbox below.

\medskip
\begin{tabular}{|p{55mm}|p{30mm}|p{55mm}|}
\hline
\textbf{Loss component} & \textbf{Estimate (EUR)} & \textbf{Basis} \\
\hline
Production loss (line down)        & & e.g.\ EUR 5\,000 / h $\times$ hours \\
\hline
Recovery + forensics               & & rebuild PLC, IR retainer day-rate \\
\hline
Regulatory (NIS2 fine ceiling)     & & up to 2\,\% group turnover \\
\hline
Customer penalties                 & & late-delivery clauses, if any \\
\hline
\textbf{LM total}                  & & \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}{G3 -- Annualised Loss Expectancy}
\[
\text{ALE} \;=\; \text{LEF} \times \text{LM} \;=\; \underline{\hspace{20mm}} \; \times \; \underline{\hspace{20mm}} \; = \; \underline{\hspace{25mm}}\ \text{EUR/yr}
\]
Compare this number against the cost of the mitigation you proposed in F1. If the mitigation costs more than three years of ALE, your CFO will push back; if it costs less than one year of ALE, you have an easy win.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{Do not put a price tag on a person.} FAIR is the right tool for production loss, IP loss, and regulatory loss. It is the \emph{wrong} tool for safety risk -- regulators (Seveso~III, IEC~61511, UK HSE) explicitly refuse the framing ``a fatality costs EUR\,X.'' For safety-bearing top events use ALARP / LOPA, document the layers, and keep reducing risk until the next euro buys disproportionately little safety.
\end{warnbox}
