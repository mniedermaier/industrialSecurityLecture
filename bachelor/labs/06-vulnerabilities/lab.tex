\labheader

\begin{goalbox}
Walk the full practitioner workflow for a single OT vulnerability: \emph{find} a published advisory, \emph{read} every field, \emph{rescore} it for a defended environment, \emph{check} your own lab PLC, and \emph{plan} a defensible response. By the end you will have a one-page brief that a plant manager could act on, and a short list of MITRE ATT\&CK for ICS techniques mapped to your findings.
\end{goalbox}

\begin{warnbox}
This lab uses real, published CVE and ICSA identifiers. None of the live commands target anything outside the local Docker stack on \texttt{172.28.0.0/16}. Never run \texttt{nmap --script vuln} or any other active probe against equipment you do not own and have written permission to test --- in OT, a single malformed packet can crash a controller and stop a process.
\end{warnbox}

\section*{Background you need (no EE knowledge required)}

You do not need to read ladder logic, wire a sensor, or understand control theory for this lab. The mental model is enough:

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item A \textbf{PLC} (Programmable Logic Controller) is a small embedded computer that reads inputs (sensors) and drives outputs (motors, valves). The one in our stack is \texttt{OpenPLC}, a software PLC.
  \item PLCs speak industrial protocols on well-known TCP ports: \textbf{Modbus/TCP on 502}, \textbf{Siemens S7 on 102}, \textbf{EtherNet/IP on 44818}, \textbf{DNP3 on 20000}. We will only touch port 502 and the OpenPLC web UI on 8080.
  \item A \textbf{CVE} (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) is a single named software bug; CVEs are scored with \textbf{CVSS}. An \textbf{ICSA} is the CISA advisory that wraps one or more CVEs in plant-owner-friendly text.
  \item Patching a PLC is not \texttt{apt-get upgrade}. It is a project. So the asset owner's first lever is usually \emph{compensating controls} (firewall, IDS, segmentation), not the firmware update itself.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Part A --- Find and decompose a real advisory}

\begin{stepbox}{1 -- Pick a CISA ICS advisory}
Open \url{https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/ics-advisories} in a browser. Filter or scroll to one of the three reference advisories below (any of them will work for the rest of the lab), or pick any current advisory whose \emph{sector} matches a real plant you could imagine working at (water, energy, manufacturing, food, pharma, transport):

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{ICSA-23-103-10} -- Siemens SIMATIC CP communication modules (CVE-2022-46140, hard-coded credential, CWE-798). Published 13 Apr 2023.
  \item \textbf{ICSA-22-228-01} -- Schneider Electric Modicon M340/M580 and related (Modipwn family, CVE-2021-22779). Published 16 Aug 2022.
  \item \textbf{ICSA-23-026-03} -- Siemens SIMATIC S7-1500 hard-coded private key (CVE-2022-38465). Published 26 Jan 2023.
\end{itemize}

Open the advisory in the browser and keep that tab open --- you will quote from it in every step below.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}{2 -- Annotate every field}
Copy the advisory into your notes and fill in this table. Quote the exact text from the advisory; do not paraphrase yet.

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{ICSA-ID} (decodes as: year, day-of-year, sequence number).
  \item \textbf{Vendor} and \textbf{product line}.
  \item \textbf{Affected versions} (the firmware band --- this is the single most load-bearing field; without it you cannot answer ``are we affected?'').
  \item \textbf{CVE IDs} listed in the advisory.
  \item \textbf{CWE} classification (e.g. CWE-798 hard-coded credentials, CWE-22 path traversal, CWE-306 missing auth).
  \item \textbf{CVSS v3.1 base score and full vector string} --- copy the vector exactly (e.g. \texttt{AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H}).
  \item \textbf{Stated impact} (one sentence in plain English).
  \item \textbf{Vendor mitigation} --- what firmware fixes this, and what compensating controls does CISA recommend until then?
  \item \textbf{Vendor PSIRT cross-reference} --- find the matching advisory on the vendor site (Siemens SSA-xxxxxx, Schneider SEVD-xxxx-xxx-xx, Rockwell PN1xxx). The CISA text always names it. Note the publication-date gap between the vendor advisory and the CISA twin.
  \item \textbf{Known exploited?} --- check whether this CVE is on CISA's \emph{Known Exploited Vulnerabilities} (KEV) catalog at \url{https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog}. A KEV listing changes everything about urgency.
\end{itemize}
\end{stepbox}

\section*{Part B --- Re-score for your environment}

\begin{stepbox}{3 -- CVSS environmental scoring, walked through}
A CVSS base score is what the \emph{vendor} thinks an average-case attacker can do. The environmental score is what \emph{you} think an attacker can do inside \emph{your} plant. The arithmetic does not change; what changes is the modifiers.

\textbf{Scenario.} Imagine the affected device sits inside an air-gapped engineering DMZ behind a one-way data diode. There is no inbound TCP. Engineering files are couriered in on a write-once USB after AV scan. The asset has high integrity importance but low confidentiality importance (the recipe is public) and moderate availability importance (the line can be re-started by hand).

Open the CVSS v3.1 calculator: \url{https://www.first.org/cvss/calculator/3.1}.

Paste the vector from Step 2 into the calculator (use the \emph{Base} fields). Then, in the \emph{Environmental} section, set:

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Modified Attack Vector (MAV) = Physical} --- with a one-way data diode the device is unreachable over a network from outside the zone; an attacker would need physical access.
  \item \textbf{Modified Attack Complexity (MAC) = High} --- the attacker has to bridge the diode somehow (rogue USB, supply-chain).
  \item \textbf{Modified Privileges Required (MPR) = High} --- only engineering staff get past the airlock.
  \item \textbf{Confidentiality Requirement (CR) = Low} --- recipe is public.
  \item \textbf{Integrity Requirement (IR) = High} --- corrupted setpoints could damage equipment.
  \item \textbf{Availability Requirement (AR) = Medium}.
\end{itemize}

Record the resulting \textbf{environmental score} and the full modified vector. For the ICSA-23-103-10 example (base 7.5, \texttt{AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:N/A:N}) the modifiers above pull the score down into the 3--5 range; for a base 9.8 RCE it lands around 4.6. Note both the old and new numbers in your brief.

\textbf{Reality check.} Re-do the calculation a second time for a different environment: the same device sitting on the cell network of a small water utility behind \emph{one} firewall with port 102 (or whatever the affected service is) reachable from the engineering VLAN. Most modifiers stay at \emph{Not Defined}; only \texttt{MAV} drops from \emph{Network} to \emph{Adjacent}. The environmental score barely moves. \textbf{This is the lesson:} CVSS-E rewards real defence-in-depth, not paper segmentation.
\end{stepbox}

\section*{Part C --- SBOM and shared components}

\begin{stepbox}{4 -- Hunt a known-bad library inside an SBOM}
An SBOM (Software Bill of Materials) lists every third-party library in a firmware image. The same buggy TCP stack ships inside dozens of vendors' products; if you only watch vendor advisories you will miss it.

Pick \emph{one} public SBOM:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item CISA SBOM resources hub: \url{https://www.cisa.gov/sbom}.
  \item Any open-source firmware's release SBOM (e.g. the Linux kernel, a popular IoT vendor on GitHub).
  \item For convenience the lab ships a small CycloneDX sample at \texttt{/labs/scripts/sboms/sample-firmware.cdx.json} inside the student container, with deliberately mixed-vintage libraries.
\end{itemize}

Search the SBOM for any of the well-known supply-chain bugs the lecture covered:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
docker compose exec student bash -lc '
  SBOM=/labs/scripts/sboms/sample-firmware.cdx.json
  echo "--- Treck TCP/IP (Ripple20, CVE-2020-11896..11914) ---"
  grep -iE "treck" "$SBOM" || echo "not found"
  echo "--- VxWorks (URGENT/11, CVE-2019-12255..12263) ---"
  grep -iE "vxworks|wind river|ipnet" "$SBOM" || echo "not found"
  echo "--- log4j (CVE-2021-44228) ---"
  grep -iE "log4j" "$SBOM" || echo "not found"
  echo "--- OpenSSL versions ---"
  grep -iE "openssl" "$SBOM" || echo "not found"
'
\end{lstlisting}

Record exactly what you found: the component name, the version, and the affected CVE range. For any hit, follow the CVE up on NVD (\url{https://nvd.nist.gov}) and note (a) whether a fixed version exists, (b) whether your hit is in the affected range, and (c) what an asset owner does next.

\textbf{If your SBOM produces no hits}, that is also a finding: report it as ``SBOM clean for the four checks I ran; recommend re-checking quarterly because new CVEs are filed against old components every week.''
\end{stepbox}

\section*{Part D --- Scan your own PLC, carefully}

\begin{stepbox}{5 -- nmap against the OpenPLC in the lab}
We will run \texttt{nmap} \emph{against the lab PLC only}, with rate-limiting on, to mimic the way an OT auditor probes a test bench. The point is not to find every bug --- it is to learn how to read \texttt{nmap} output critically and to separate genuine findings from script noise.

\textbf{Step 5a -- Banner, version, open services.}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
docker compose exec student nmap -sT -Pn \
  --max-rate 20 --scan-delay 100ms \
  -p 80,102,443,502,4840,8080,20000,44818,20256 \
  -sV 172.28.0.10
\end{lstlisting}
Record every open port, the service name nmap printed, and the banner. The OpenPLC stack should show 502/tcp and 8080/tcp at minimum.

\textbf{Step 5b -- Protocol-specific scripts.}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
docker compose exec student nmap -Pn -p 502  \
  --script modbus-discover --script-args='modbus-discover.aggressive=true' \
  172.28.0.10
docker compose exec student nmap -Pn -p 102  --script s7-info  172.28.0.10
docker compose exec student nmap -Pn -p 44818 --script enip-info 172.28.0.10
\end{lstlisting}
Note: \texttt{s7-info} and \texttt{enip-info} will report nothing useful here (we do not run a Siemens PLC or an EtherNet/IP device), but the \emph{absence} of output is part of the lesson: protocol-specific scripts only fire on the protocol they target.

\textbf{Step 5c -- The \texttt{vuln} category, with eyes open.}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
docker compose exec student nmap -sT -Pn \
  --max-rate 20 --scan-delay 100ms \
  --script vuln -p 80,502,8080 172.28.0.10 \
  | tee /tmp/lab06-nmap-vuln.txt
\end{lstlisting}
\texttt{nmap --script vuln} runs every NSE script tagged \texttt{vuln}. Expect a long output and a mix of:

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item Genuine findings (the OpenPLC web UI is HTTP-only, has weak session handling, and accepts default credentials in some versions --- valid concerns).
  \item Plausible-but-needs-verification findings (e.g.\ outdated TLS, default cookies). Triage each before reporting.
  \item False positives where a script's heuristic guessed wrong (e.g.\ a generic CVE flagged on a service that does not actually run the affected code).
\end{itemize}

For each \texttt{nmap} finding, tag it \textbf{Confirmed}, \textbf{Plausible}, or \textbf{Noise}, and write one sentence saying \emph{why} you tagged it that way. ``It is a CVE'' is not why; ``the affected version range matches the banner I recorded in Step 0'' is.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{warnbox}
The flags \texttt{--max-rate 20} and \texttt{--scan-delay 100ms} are deliberately conservative. Even against a software PLC they keep traffic gentle; against real hardware you would go slower still and only after a documented bench result. Never replace them with \texttt{-T4} or \texttt{-T5} on anything that resembles a real PLC.
\end{warnbox}

\section*{Part E --- Make the findings actionable}

\begin{stepbox}{6 -- Map findings to MITRE ATT\&CK for ICS}
Open \url{https://attack.mitre.org/matrices/ics/} and pick the techniques that best describe how an attacker would exploit each finding from Steps 2--5. Use the official short IDs (e.g. \texttt{T0855} Unauthorized Command Message). Common mappings for this lab:

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Modbus write without auth} on 502 (Lab 04 already proved this works) $\rightarrow$ \textbf{T0855} \emph{Unauthorized Command Message}, with \textbf{T0836} \emph{Modify Parameter} as the impact-level technique.
  \item \textbf{Hard-coded credentials in firmware} (CWE-798 advisory) $\rightarrow$ \textbf{T0859} \emph{Valid Accounts}, supported by \textbf{T0812} \emph{Default Credentials}.
  \item \textbf{Information disclosure via web UI} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{T0846} \emph{Remote System Discovery}, \textbf{T0888} \emph{Remote System Information Discovery}.
  \item \textbf{Unauthenticated firmware upload} (Modipwn-class) $\rightarrow$ \textbf{T0857} \emph{System Firmware} as the technique, \textbf{T0809} \emph{Data Destruction} or \textbf{T0816} \emph{Device Restart/Shutdown} as the impact.
  \item \textbf{Network reconnaissance} you just did with nmap $\rightarrow$ \textbf{T0840} \emph{Network Connection Enumeration}, \textbf{T0846} \emph{Remote System Discovery}.
\end{itemize}

For \emph{each} finding from Steps 2 and 5, produce one line: \texttt{Finding -- T-ID -- Technique name -- Mitigation}. A mitigation may be a firewall rule, an IDS signature, a firmware patch, a network re-segmentation, or a documented procedure. ``Buy a product'' is not a mitigation; ``deny inbound TCP/502 from the office VLAN at the firewall between L3 and L2'' is.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}{7 -- This week, this quarter}
Translate everything above into the format a plant manager understands. Produce two short lists:

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{This week (compensating controls):} firewall rules, IDS signatures, monitoring, account hygiene, removal of unnecessary services. These should require zero downtime.
  \item \textbf{This quarter (durable fix):} firmware update plan, change-window scheduling, bench-test plan, rollback plan, post-change re-assessment. Cite IEC 62443-2-3 if you want the asset-owner cycle by name.
\end{itemize}

Limit each list to five lines. Brevity is the deliverable.
\end{stepbox}

\section*{Part F --- Stretch (optional)}

\begin{stepbox}{8 -- One CVE in depth (stretch)}
Pick \emph{one} of the historic ICS / embedded supply-chain advisories below and read the primary source (Armis, Forescout, or the vendor PSIRT --- not a vendor blog). Write a one-paragraph executive summary aimed at a board member: what broke, who was exposed, what the lasting lesson is.

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{URGENT/11} -- Armis, 2019. Eleven bugs in Wind River VxWorks' IPnet TCP/IP stack, six of them RCE. Most-cited reference: Armis, \emph{URGENT/11}, July 2019.
  \item \textbf{Ripple20} -- JSOF / Forescout, 2020. Nineteen bugs in Treck Inc.'s TCP/IP stack, embedded in hundreds of vendors' firmware. CVE-2020-11896 through CVE-2020-11914.
  \item \textbf{CODESYS CoDe16} -- Microsoft \& CODESYS, 2023. Sixteen vulnerabilities in the CODESYS V3 runtime affecting many vendors who OEM the stack.
  \item \textbf{CVE-2022-22094} -- VxWorks 6.9 and 7.0 IPnet, ICMPv6 information disclosure. A small bug that propagated through every device shipped on the affected stacks.
\end{itemize}

In the paragraph, name: (a) the affected component, (b) the number of downstream products known to be affected, (c) the year of disclosure, (d) why a sole vendor patch was not enough, (e) what SBOM would have changed.
\end{stepbox}

\section*{Self-check}

Before handing in, make sure you can answer each of these in one sentence:

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item What does the ICSA-ID encode, and why does the gap between vendor PSIRT and CISA matter?
  \item Why did the CVSS base score of your advisory change so much (or so little) under the environmental modifiers?
  \item Which of your nmap findings would you still trust if the scan ran for ten seconds instead of two minutes? Which would you not?
  \item Which mitigation from Step 7 would survive a firmware rollback? Which would not?
  \item Which two ATT\&CK techniques cover the largest number of your findings?
\end{itemize}
