\noindent\textbf{Marking note.} Each solution box gives a sample answer plus
\emph{rubric hints}: what a good answer must contain, common mistakes, and the
standards a student is expected to cite. The numerical values used in LOPA and
FAIR sub-tasks are \emph{indicative} -- accept any defensible range that the
student justifies.

\vspace{0.6em}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 7.1 -- 5$\times$5 Risk Matrix.}
A defensible policy: green = (likelihood + consequence) $\le 3$;
yellow = $4$--$5$; orange = $6$; red = $\ge 7$. Sample placement:
(a) historian ransomware -- Likely / Moderate (yellow): annoying but no
process impact if backups work; (b) spoofed Modbus write to dosing
setpoint -- Possible / Major (orange): public-health consequence;
(c) stolen laptop with cached HMI password -- Possible / Major (orange):
direct path to setpoints; (d) cellular link loss 30~min -- Likely /
Minor (yellow): local PLC keeps running; (e) vendor session left open
weekend -- Possible / Catastrophic (red): unsupervised privileged access
to safety-relevant equipment.
\textbf{Rubric:} student must (i) state the colour rule explicitly,
(ii) justify each placement with reference to the OT consequence
dimensions, (iii) recognise (e) as the highest because of unsupervised
privileged access. Common mistake: putting (a) in red because
``ransomware sounds bad'' without considering containment.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 7.2 -- Bow-Tie (chlorine setpoint).}
Sample causes (left): C1~insider with HMI rights changes setpoint;
C2~Modbus write injection from compromised engineering laptop;
C3~operator typo during shift handover (human error).
Consequences (right): K1~under-chlorination $\to$ microbial breakthrough
(safety / public health); K2~over-chlorination $\to$ taste complaints \&
EPA / drinking-water-regulator reporting (regulatory); K3~plant shutdown
to flush and re-sample (production).
Preventive barriers: P1~role-based access on HMI with named accounts
(procedural / technical); P2~Modbus write allow-list at the IDS / firewall
on the conduit between HMI and PLC (technical).
Mitigating barriers: M1~independent online residual analyser with hardware
alarm and auto-shutoff (technical / physical); M2~written customer-notification
SOP $\le 1$~h after detection (procedural).
\textbf{Rubric:} barriers must be \emph{independent} of the cause they
protect against; a barrier that runs on the same compromised HMI does not
count. Good answers explicitly label each barrier (procedural / technical /
physical).
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 7.3 -- Cyber-HAZOP on P-101.}
\emph{No flow:} attacker writes pump-speed setpoint to 0 via Modbus
$\to$ loss of cooling, exotherm in R-201.
\emph{More flow:} attacker raises setpoint above design $\to$ pump
cavitation, mechanical damage, possible loss of suction.
\emph{Less flow:} attacker degrades setpoint slowly (ramp below alarm
rate-of-change) $\to$ creeping temperature rise undetected by the
$\pm 20\%$ deviation alarm.
\emph{Reverse flow:} attacker disables check-valve actuator and stops
pump $\to$ reactor contents back-flow into supply, contamination.
\emph{As well as:} attacker injects a spoofed ``flow OK'' value in
parallel with the real one $\to$ operator sees normal reading while
real flow is zero (loss of view).
\textbf{Rubric:} answers must distinguish the \emph{cyber} cause from a
random equipment failure (e.g.\ ``setpoint written by attacker'', not
``pump fails''). Cite IEC~61882 for the guide words and
ISA~TR84.00.09 for the cyber adaptation. ``Loss of view'' (the
``as well as'' case) is the subtle one and earns full marks.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 7.4 -- LOPA Calculation.}
Credited IPLs with typical PFDs (per CCPS LOPA):
BPCS flow loop -- \emph{not} an IPL because the initiating event is its
failure (independence rule); high-T alarm + operator response
$\text{PFD}=1\times 10^{-1}$; SIS SIL-2 trip $\text{PFD}=1\times 10^{-2}$;
PSV $\text{PFD}=1\times 10^{-2}$. Residual:
$f_\text{mit} = 0.1 \cdot 10^{-1} \cdot 10^{-2} \cdot 10^{-2}
              = 1\times 10^{-6}/\text{year}$
(one event per million years -- well within most company appetites for a
runaway exotherm).
\textbf{Key assumption:} the three layers are \emph{independent}. If the
SIS shares the same engineering workstation as the BPCS, a single cyber
compromise removes both layers and the math is wrong; IEC~61511 mandates
this independence.
\textbf{Rubric:} full marks require (i) excluding the BPCS loop as an
IPL because it is the initiating event source, (ii) multiplying PFDs
correctly, (iii) naming the independence assumption. Common mistake:
crediting the BPCS twice.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 7.5 -- CCE Phase~1.}
Sample top-three unacceptable outcomes for a speciality-chemicals site:
\textbf{(1)}~toxic release from a storage tank into the neighbouring
community -- ranked first because it is the only outcome that can
produce off-site fatalities and the only one that almost certainly
ends the licence to operate.
\textbf{(2)}~runaway exotherm in a batch reactor causing on-site fatality
and a multi-week shutdown -- ranked second: catastrophic to staff and
the business but contained to the fence-line.
\textbf{(3)}~rail-loading mis-routing causing wrong-product delivery
to a customer (e.g.\ a strong acid labelled as solvent) -- ranked
third: severe regulatory / liability exposure and brand damage but
recoverable.
\textbf{Next phase:} CCE Phase~2 -- \emph{System-of-systems analysis}
-- maps the cyber / digital and supply-chain dependencies for each of
the prioritised consequences (what would have to fail to realise
outcome 1?).
\textbf{Rubric:} reject lists of $>5$ consequences (Phase~1 is about
ruthless prioritisation). Accept reasonable ranking criteria as long as
they are explicit (off-site harm, irreversibility, licence to operate).
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 7.6 -- Risk Response.}
\textbf{Transfer.} Right: cyber-insurance to cover business-interruption
loss after ransomware on a corporate ERP (large, low-probability,
non-safety). Wrong: transferring a safety risk (``insurance will pay
if someone dies'') -- the residual harm to humans is not transferred;
mitigate or avoid instead.
\textbf{Accept.} Right: a legacy serial RS-232 link to a stand-alone
analyser, air-gapped, with no remote write path -- documented,
signed-off, reviewed annually. Wrong: accepting an Internet-exposed
HMI because ``no incident yet'' -- this is acceptance by default,
not by decision; avoid (remove the exposure).
\textbf{Mitigate.} Right: deploy MFA, segmentation and EDR on the
engineering-workstation tier to reduce both likelihood and consequence
of ransomware. Wrong: mitigating around a fundamentally unsafe
practice such as routine remote programming of an SIS during operation
-- the right answer is \emph{avoid} (key-switch in RUN, no remote writes).
\textbf{Avoid.} Right: decommission the unused dial-up modem on the
PLC. Wrong: ``avoiding'' a critical vendor remote-support tunnel that
the operator depends on for emergency fixes -- you lose recovery
capability; mitigate (brokered, recorded, MFA) instead.
\textbf{Rubric:} students must give a concrete OT artefact for each,
not generic IT examples. Marking the wrong-choice case is the
discriminator.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 7.7 -- Merck v.\ Ace American.}
The insurer denied USD~1.4~bn of Merck's NotPetya loss by invoking the
``hostile or warlike action'' exclusion, arguing that NotPetya was a
Russian state-attributed attack on Ukraine and therefore an act of war.
The New Jersey Appellate Division held that the exclusion clause, as
written in Merck's all-risk property policy, had historically been
understood to cover \emph{kinetic} military action; it could not be
stretched, without notice to the insured, to cover a state-attributed
cyber operation in a peace-time commercial network. Merck was therefore
covered.
\textbf{Consequences for an OT operator in 2026:} (i) modern policies have
been re-written with explicit ``cyber-war'' or
``state-attributed attack'' exclusions (Lloyd's market bulletin 2022)
-- read them carefully; (ii) expect attribution clauses that defer to
government attribution statements (NCSC, CISA), sub-limits for
critical-infrastructure operators, and required control baselines
(MFA, EDR, immutable backups) as preconditions to cover.
\textbf{Why insurance is not a substitute for safety mitigation:}
insurance pays money after the fact; it does not bring back staff,
reverse a toxic release, or restore a regulator's trust. For any
risk whose consequence dimension is \emph{safety} or
\emph{environment}, ALARP / inherent-safety reasoning applies and the
risk must be driven down regardless of insurability.
\textbf{Rubric:} full marks require recognising both the policy-wording
point and the safety-vs-financial-loss distinction.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 7.8 -- FAIR Estimate (historian ransomware).}
Sample values (defensible, not authoritative):
\textbf{TEF} = 2 / year -- ransomware actors reach Internet-exposed
corporate assets routinely; reaching the historian requires crossing
into Level~3, so the rate is lower than for IT (use a most-likely value
with a range, e.g.\ 1--5/year).
\textbf{Vulnerability} = 0.10 -- current controls (network segmentation,
EDR, monthly offline backup, MFA on jump host) defeat most actions;
roughly one in ten attempts succeed end-to-end.
\textbf{Loss Magnitude:} primary -- $\approx$~EUR~80{,}000
(IR retainer EUR~30k, internal labour EUR~20k, restore + revalidation
EUR~30k); secondary -- $\approx$~EUR~70{,}000 (regulator notification,
late-reporting fines, customer credits) $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{LM}
$\approx$~EUR~150{,}000.
\textbf{Loss Exposure} $= 2 \times 0.10 \times 150{,}000$
$= \text{EUR}~30{,}000$ / year.
\textbf{Biggest uncertainty:} the \emph{Vulnerability} factor -- it
depends on the realism of the offline backup test; if backups have
never been restored end-to-end, the true Vuln is closer to 0.5 and the
loss exposure jumps to EUR~150{,}000 / year.
\textbf{Rubric:} accept any internally consistent number set, but the
student must (i) show the arithmetic, (ii) name a primary and a
secondary loss category, (iii) identify the dominant uncertainty.
Reject single point estimates with no range or assumption.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 7.9 -- Risk Register Row.}
Sample row for the lab:
\textbf{ID:} \texttt{R-OT-014}.
\textbf{Description:} ``Default credentials on lab OpenPLC web HMI
permit unauthenticated setpoint writes from any host on the lab VLAN,
risking unintended physical actuation of the demo conveyor.''
\textbf{Likelihood:} \emph{Likely} (4/5) -- the credentials are
documented in the vendor manual and known to all students.
\textbf{Consequence:} \emph{Moderate} (3/5) -- demo equipment can pinch
fingers; no chemical or process risk in this lab.
\textbf{Owner:} Lab supervisor (Prof.\ X).
\textbf{Treatment:} \emph{Mitigate} -- change default password,
restrict HMI to lab-host /24, enable session timeout (10~min).
\textbf{Residual rating:} \emph{Unlikely / Minor} (yellow) after
treatment.
\textbf{Review date:} 30~September~2026 (start of winter semester).
\textbf{Rubric:} reject rows with vague ownership (``IT'') or no
review date. The residual rating must be \emph{lower} than the
inherent rating, and the student must say so explicitly.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{solutionbox}
\textbf{Exercise 7.10 -- False Precision.}
\textbf{What is wrong:} (i)~two decimals on a 10-point qualitative
scale imply $\sim$1\,\% measurement resolution that the inputs do not
support; (ii)~no confidence interval / range is given, so the score
hides its own uncertainty; (iii)~the weighting and aggregation of
factors is opaque -- a single ``score'' suggests the factors are
independent and commensurable, which they almost never are.
\textbf{Questions to the vendor:}
(1)~What is the underlying data (CVE feed? asset inventory? telemetry?)
and how recent is it?
(2)~How are factor weights chosen and have they been calibrated against
real incident data?
(3)~Are the factors statistically independent, or are you
double-counting (e.g.\ ``patch level'' and ``CVE count'' on the same PLC)?
(4)~What is the confidence interval / sensitivity of the score? Show
me the same calculation with $\pm 1$ standard deviation on each input.
\textbf{Defensible alternative:} report a NIST~SP~800-30 semi-quantitative
band (Very Low / Low / Moderate / High / Very High) with the rationale,
or a FAIR loss-exposure range in money with explicit min / most-likely / max
values (Jones \& Freund). Either makes the uncertainty visible
instead of laundering it through a false-precision number.
\textbf{Rubric:} students must hit all three methodological issues in
part~(1) and at least three of the four vendor questions; bonus marks
for naming sensitivity analysis or Monte~Carlo as the way to surface
the confidence interval.
\end{solutionbox}
